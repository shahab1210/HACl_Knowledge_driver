# Vibration Sensor in Industrial IoT Fault Diagnosis

## 1. What the Sensor Measures
An industrial IoT vibration sensor measures dynamic oscillatory motion, acceleration, or velocity of machinery frames, bearing housings, or structural supports. Transducers typically employ piezoelectric accelerometers, piezoresistive microelectromechanical systems (MEMS), or eddy-current proximity probes. The sensor captures cyclical mechanical motion along one or more orthogonal axes (horizontal, vertical, axial) and converts mechanical acceleration (measured in g or m/s²), velocity (in mm/s or in/s RMS), or peak-to-peak displacement (in micrometers or mils) into analog or digital telemetry.

## 2. What a Normal Reading Generally Represents
A normal vibration reading indicates smooth, balanced rotational dynamics where dynamic forces generated by rotating masses, shaft couplings, and fluid impellers are well within designed mechanical tolerances. Under healthy conditions, the equipment exhibits baseline vibration levels conforming to established standards such as ISO 10816 / ISO 20816 Class I, II, or III vibration severity limits.

> **Important Operational Note:**
> Actual limits depend on the equipment specification, sensor location, operating conditions, and manufacturer recommendations.

For instance, a rigidly mounted small-to-medium motor (up to 15 kW) operating in good condition typically exhibits an overall vibration velocity below 1.8 to 2.8 mm/s RMS, whereas values exceeding 4.5 mm/s RMS require alert notification, and levels above 7.1 to 11 mm/s RMS demand immediate shutdown.

## 3. What Abnormal Readings Can Indicate
Elevated or irregular vibration readings indicate abnormal kinetic forces, mechanical degradation, or hydraulic turbulence:
- **Mass Unbalance:** High sinusoidal 1x rotational speed (1x RPM) vibration caused by uneven weight distribution on fans, rotors, or impellers.
- **Shaft Misalignment:** Predominantly 1x and 2x RPM vibration, frequently accompanied by strong axial directional vibration across couplings.
- **Mechanical Looseness:** Harmonic series (1x, 2x, 3x, 4x, etc.) with sub-synchronous components caused by loose hold-down bolts, worn housings, or soft foot.
- **Bearing Raceway Degradation:** High-frequency impact spikes and non-synchronous bearing fault frequencies (BPFO, BPFI, BSF, FTF).
- **Hydraulic Turbulence and Cavitation:** Random, high-frequency broadband noise floor elevation caused by collapsing vapor bubbles or fluid recirculation.
- **Sensor Detachment or Saturated Rail:** Sensor loose from stud mount, broken cable, or internal clipping resulting in false high/low readings.

## 4. Which Faults the Sensor Can Help Identify
Vibration is one of the most versatile and sensitive indicators of mechanical and hydraulic degradation:
- **[FAULT-005: Excessive Motor Vibration](../faults/motor-vibration.md):** Overall RMS velocity elevation across fundamental and harmonic rotational frequencies.
- **[FAULT-002: Bearing Wear](../faults/bearing-wear.md):** High-frequency impulsive acceleration peaks, elevated crest factor, and bearing fault frequency harmonics.
- **[FAULT-001: Bearing Overheating](../faults/bearing-overheating.md):** High friction carpet noise and elevated overall vibration accompanying thermal rise.
- **[FAULT-006: Pump Cavitation](../faults/pump-cavitation.md):** High-energy broadband vibration spikes (> 1 kHz to ultrasonic range) caused by imploding vapor cavities.
- **[FAULT-004: Motor Overcurrent](../faults/motor-overcurrent.md):** Corroborates whether overcurrent stems from mechanical binding/misalignment or pure electrical issues.
- **[FAULT-010: Sensor Anomaly](../faults/sensor-anomaly.md):** Flatline zero reading or saturated rail when the accelerometer or wiring fails.

## 5. Limitations of Using That Sensor Alone
Despite its diagnostic power, vibration monitoring cannot function as a standalone diagnostic tool without corroborating sensor data:
- **Root Cause Ambiguity:** An elevated overall vibration level indicates mechanical distress but cannot confirm whether the underlying cause is an electrical fault (broken rotor bar), mechanical wear (bearing raceway spall), or hydraulic instability (pump cavitation) without detailed spectral analysis and multi-sensor correlation.
- **Mounting Dependency:** Sensor placement, mounting method (magnetic base, stud mount, adhesive, or hand-held probe), and surface cleanliness heavily influence high-frequency response and measurement repeatability.
- **Insensitivity to Pure Thermal or Electrical Degradation:** Pure electrical winding degradation, insulation drying, minor phase unbalance, or steady-state fluid blockage will not generate notable vibration signatures until physical damage has already occurred.
- **Cross-Coupled Background Noise:** Vibration from adjacent equipment, piping resonance, or plant structural vibrations can transmit through baseplates, causing false alarms on a healthy machine.
